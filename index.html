About

From AI research to AI in organizations

My work has evolved across speech and biometric AI, computer vision and medical AI, enterprise machine learning, Generative AI, retrieval-augmented generation, and agentic AI. Across these settings, I have become increasingly interested in what happens when AI moves beyond an isolated model and becomes part of a larger system, workflow, or decision process.

My current research direction focuses on AI-supported organizational decision-making: how AI can combine prediction, evidence, uncertainty, intervention, and human–AI responsibility to support better organizational outcomes. My industry experience informs this work by exposing me to the practical constraints that determine whether an AI capability can create value in a real organization.

Research

AI-supported organizational decision-making

I am interested in building AI systems that become part of organizational decision processes rather than simply producing predictions or generated information. My research connects AI/ML methods with questions that matter to organizations: what is happening, what evidence supports a decision, where intervention creates value, and what an organization should do next.

01 · RELIABLE DECISION SUPPORT

Prediction + evidence + uncertainty

AI systems that understand process state, identify emerging risks, retrieve relevant evidence, and communicate uncertainty so that decisions have a reliable basis for action.

02 · VALUE-AWARE INTERVENTION

From risk to action

Decision systems that consider expected intervention effects, cost, and resource availability to identify where intervention is most likely to create meaningful value.

03 · ADAPTIVE HUMAN–AI DECISION-MAKING

Appropriate autonomy

Human–AI systems that determine when to recommend, escalate, defer, or execute based on uncertainty, risk, impact, evidence, and reversibility.

Together, these directions connect organizational state and prediction with evidence, intervention, and action.

Teaching

Teaching philosophy

“Ultimately, I want students to leave my classroom with more than knowledge: the confidence to question, the ability to think independently, and the curiosity to keep learning.”

I think of learning as a process that develops in layers: starting with intuition and examples, moving through implementation and experimentation, and then reaching deeper technical understanding. I use meaningful problems to create the need for concepts, and I value questions, mistakes, and debugging as part of learning.

In an era where AI can generate answers and solutions almost instantly, I believe students need to develop the ability to question, evaluate, and justify those solutions rather than simply accept them.
